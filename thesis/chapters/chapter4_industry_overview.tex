\chapter{TAMIL FILM INDUSTRY -- A HISTORICAL AND CONTEMPORARY OVERVIEW}
\label{ch:industry_overview}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:overview_intro}

This chapter situates the empirical chapters that follow by providing
a structural-historical account of the Tamil film industry.  It
traces the industry's evolution from the silent era to the
pre-pandemic equilibrium of 2019; describes the institutional
architecture of production, distribution, exhibition, music and OTT
on the eve of the pandemic; and presents the secondary-data
trajectory of theatrical revenue, release volumes and OTT
penetration that frame the primary-survey findings of
Chapters~\ref{ch:viewership} and~\ref{ch:industry_reconfig}.  The
chapter draws on \citet{baskaran1996,baskaran2009,velayutham2008,
nakassis2017,prasad2014,kohlikhandekar2013}, supplemented by
FICCI--EY \citeyearpar{ficciey2018,ficciey2019,ficciey2020,
ficciey2021,ficciey2022,ficciey2023,ficciey2024}, Ormax
\citeyearpar{ormax2022,ormax2023,ormax2024}, KPMG
\citeyearpar{kpmg2019,kpmg2021,kpmg2022,kpmg2023}, Statista
\citeyearpar{statista2019,statista2023,statista2024} and the
Tamil-film trade press.

\section{Origins and the Silent Era (1916--1931)}
\label{sec:silent}

Although moving images had been screened in Madras Presidency from
1897, indigenous production in Tamil began in the 1910s
\citep{baskaran1996}.  R.\,Nataraja Mudaliar's \emph{Keechaka
Vadham} (1918) is widely regarded as the first feature-length film
made in South India, drawing on a \emph{Mahabharata} episode and
exhibited in tents and temporary structures in Madras.  The silent
era was dominated by mythologicals and adaptations from sthala
puranas (local legends), produced by small studios such as Star of
the East Films and Associated Films \citep{baskaran1996,
hardgrave1973}.

By the late 1920s, the industry had developed a circuit of
single-screen tent-and-roof exhibition spaces in Madras, Madurai,
Tiruchirappalli and Coimbatore, supplied by a handful of imported
production companies \citep{baskaran1996,velayutham2008}.  Even
during this nascent phase, Tamil cinema's distinctive grammar of
mythological-political allegory, large-scale theatrical performance
and the active engagement of urban audiences was discernible.

\section{The Talkies and the Studio Era (1931--1949)}
\label{sec:talkies}

The transition to sound began with H.\,M.\,Reddy's \emph{Kalidas}
(1931), the first sound film with Tamil dialogue and Telugu songs
\citep{baskaran2009}.  The 1930s saw the rise of dedicated sound
studios -- Modern Theatres at Salem (1937), AVM Studios at Karaikudi
(1945, later Madras), Gemini Studios (1940) -- which industrialised
production and consolidated a recognisable Tamil cinematic style
characterised by long musical sequences, declamatory dialogue and
strong literary sources from Sangam-era classics through Bharathi's
poetry to contemporary stage drama \citep{baskaran1996,
ramaswamy1997}.

Two features of this period are pertinent for the present thesis.
First, the studio era institutionalised \textbf{music as a
production-financing instrument}: gramophone-record sales of
soundtracks frequently funded a non-trivial share of production
budgets.  Second, the studio era cemented \textbf{vernacular
literary} respectability for cinema, drawing in writers such as
Pa.\,Pendurai (Kalki), C.\,N.\,Annadurai and M.\,Karunanidhi --- the
last two of whom would, two decades later, become Chief Ministers of
the State \citep{pandian1992,hardgrave1973}.

\section{The Dravidian-Political Phase (1949--1969)}
\label{sec:dravidian}

The post-war decades saw Tamil cinema become a vector of Dravidian
nationalist politics.  C.\,N.\,Annadurai's screenplays for
\emph{Velaikkari} (1949) and \emph{Or Iravu} (1951) established the
template of socially conscious, anti-caste melodrama that defined
the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam's (DMK) cinematic project
\citep{hardgrave1973,pandian1992}.  Sivaji Ganesan emerged as the
era's outstanding actor, while M.\,G.\,Ramachandran (MGR) cultivated
the persona of the working-class hero whose screen \emph{character}
was inseparable from his political identity.  Pandian
\citeyearpar{pandian1992} provides the canonical reading of how MGR
constructed his political base through the cinematic image.

This period laid down two structural features that endure into the
post-pandemic moment: a politically organised \textbf{fan-club}
infrastructure (rasikar mandram) which would later be co-opted into
opening-day box-office mobilisation \citep{nakassis2017,
pandian1992}; and a normative expectation that major stars would
play \emph{political} roles, on or off screen, that no other
sub-national cinema in India institutionalised to the same degree.

\section{The Stardom Era (1970s--1990s)}
\label{sec:stardom}

The 1970s brought the emergence of a multistar economy.  MGR's
political ascendancy created space for the so-called ``second
generation'' of stars: Rajinikanth (debut 1975), Kamal Haasan
(starting 1959 as a child artist; lead from 1973 onwards) and a
trajectory of senior character-leads.  The 1980s saw the
institutionalisation of \textbf{star-fee escalation}, the
emergence of dedicated post-production houses and -- critically --
the development of \textbf{pre-sale economics}: producers could
finance new films by selling territorial rights, satellite rights
and audio rights months in advance \citep{kohlikhandekar2013,
ravindran2018}.

By the 1990s, Tamil cinema was generating an estimated 130--160
films a year and exporting to a Tamil diaspora in Sri Lanka,
Malaysia, Singapore and -- increasingly -- the Gulf, the United
Kingdom and North America \citep{baskaran2009}.  The Tamil
star-system had taken its modern form: a small handful of top-tier
stars commanding fees that could account for 30--40\% of a film's
budget; an A-list of approximately fifteen actors; a B-list of
fifty-plus; and a long tail of character actors and supporting
performers.

\section{Industrial Modernisation in the 2000s}
\label{sec:2000s}

The 2000s were marked by structural modernisation.  Multiplex
construction in Chennai began in earnest with Sathyam Cinemas'
re-launch (2002, later acquired by SPI / PVR) and AGS Cinemas
(opened 2009), supplemented by the entry of pan-India operators
PVR, Inox and Cinepolis \citep{kohlikhandekar2013,ficciey2018}.
The cumulative effect was a steady premiumisation of the Chennai
market alongside the persistence of a strong single-screen segment
in Tier-2 and Tier-3 towns.

The 2000s also saw the rise of pan-India digital projection,
satellite-rights aggregation by Sun Network's KTV / Sun TV / Vijay
TV, and a measurable improvement in technical quality (visual
effects, sound design, post-production).  Several films of the
period -- Mani Ratnam's \emph{Kannathil Muthamittal} (2002), Bala's
\emph{Pithamagan} (2003), Kamal Haasan's \emph{Anbe Sivam} (2003),
Vetrimaaran's \emph{Polladhavan} (2007) -- expanded the genre
repertoire of mainstream Tamil cinema beyond mass-action and
family-drama into political-realism, character-driven thrillers and
investigative subjects \citep{velayutham2008,nakassis2017}.

\section{The 2010s: Multiplexes, Music and Markets}
\label{sec:2010s}

The 2010s were a decade of capital intensification.  Three
trajectories matter for our argument.

\textit{Budget escalation.}  Production budgets for top-tier
star-led films rose from a band of INR 35--60 crore in the early
2010s to INR 100--250 crore by 2018--2019, driven by escalating
star-fees, VFX-heavy spectacles, and rising marketing spends
\citep{ravindran2018,kohlikhandekar2013}.

\textit{Music-video and digital marketing.}  Audio launches became
hybrid live and televised events, music-video YouTube release
became a primary marketing instrument, and trailer-launch metrics
(views, shares) became part of the trade-press conversation
\citep{velayutham2019,filmcompanion2019audio}.

\textit{Markets and sub-territories.}  The geographic pre-sale
economy matured: Tamil Nadu was sub-divided into Chennai, North,
South, Mid-Mofussil, Madurai, Salem--Erode, Kongu (Coimbatore) and
Tirunelveli circuits, each with its own distributors; Karnataka,
Kerala and Andhra Pradesh / Telangana were treated as adjacent
territories; and the Overseas market (US--Canada, UK, Singapore,
Malaysia, Gulf) was rationalised through specialised distributors
\citep{kohlikhandekar2013}.

The trajectory exhibits four distinguishable phases.  (i) A stable

followed by a partial 2021 recovery to 43\% of the pre-pandemic

(iv) A new equilibrium (2023--2025) at INR 1{,}600--1{,}800 crore,
13--53\% above the pre-pandemic baseline in nominal terms but with
materially different revenue-mix composition (Chapter~6).

Three observations are important.  First, the post-2022 theatrical
volume has stabilised in the 218--224 range, slightly below the
2019 peak of 233 but well above the 2020 trough.  Second, direct-to-OTT
releases have grown from 12 in 2018 to 178 in 2025, a roughly 15$\times$
increase, with the steepest growth in the immediate post-pandemic
years.  Third -- and crucially for the argument of this thesis --
the two trajectories are not inversely related.  The growth of
direct-to-OTT did not come at the expense of theatrical releases;
rather, the two channels have grown roughly in parallel, suggesting
\textbf{complementarity rather than substitution}.

\section{The Music Industry and the Audio Launch}
\label{sec:music}

The audio launch is a distinctive Tamil cinematic institution.  The
public release of a film's soundtrack -- typically two to four
months before the film's theatrical release -- is staged as a
ticketed or televised event drawing crowds of 8{,}000--40{,}000 in
auditoria such as the Nehru Indoor Stadium, the YMCA Grounds and
purpose-built lawn venues
\citep{velayutham2008,filmcompanion2019audio}.

The music industry's commercial structure was historically
gramophone-physical and cassette-tape based; in the 2000s it
migrated to digital aggregation by labels such as Sony Music India,
T-Series, Sa Re Ga Ma and the Tamil-focused Think Music.
Post-2015, music-rights revenues have been decisively shaped by
Spotify, Apple Music, JioSaavn, Wynk, Gaana and YouTube
audio-and-video streaming.  Although digital music revenue has
grown rapidly, the audio-launch event has retained its cultural and
marketing significance even as its monetisation has shifted toward
the streaming long-tail.  The post-pandemic re-organisation of the
audio-launch event into a hybrid live-and-streamed format is one of
the supply-side reconfigurations documented in
Chapter~\ref{ch:industry_reconfig}.

\section{The Star Economy and Fan Clubs}
\label{sec:starecon}

The star economy of Tamil cinema is institutionally organised
through fan clubs (rasikar mandram).  Major stars maintain
hierarchically structured federations of fan clubs at district,
constituency and ward levels, totalling -- depending on the source
and definition -- between 25{,}000 and 50{,}000 active units across
Tamil Nadu \citep{nakassis2017,pandian1992}.  Fan clubs perform a
range of functions: opening-day mobilisation; ticket-bulk-booking;
publicity; charity drives in the star's name; and, frequently, the
political organisation of the star's followers.

The economic significance of this infrastructure is non-trivial.
Fan-club mobilisation can deliver opening-day occupancies of
85--95\% in Tamil-Nadu single-screens for major star releases
\citep{ormax2023}, generating disproportionate first-day-first-show
(FDFS) revenue.  The post-pandemic reconfiguration of fan-club
behaviour, in particular the digitalisation of fan-club coordination
and the (modest) decline in pure-mobilisation effectiveness, is
documented in Chapter~\ref{ch:industry_reconfig}.

\section{Trade Bodies and Associations}
\label{sec:trade_bodies}

The Tamil film industry's institutional infrastructure includes:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
\item The \textbf{Tamil Film Producers Council (TFPC)} -- the apex
      producers' body, with negotiating responsibility on
      theatre-rental terms, distributor-share splits and tickling-tax
      issues with the Government of Tamil Nadu.
\item The \textbf{Tamil Film Active Producers Association (TFAPA)}
      -- a competing producers' body that has played a role in
      strikes and contractual standardisation.
\item The \textbf{Film Employees Federation of South India (FEFSI)}
      -- representing technicians and crew.
\item The \textbf{Tamil Nadu Theatre Owners' Federation} -- the
      apex body for single-screen exhibitors.
\item Star-specific associations -- the Nadigar Sangam (South Indian
      Artistes' Association), the Director's Union, etc.
\end{itemize}

These bodies have historically negotiated industrial-action episodes
including the 2017--18 producer-distributor strike (over digital
service charges), the 2018 satellite-rights dispute and the 2020
COVID-shutdown reopening protocols.  Their post-pandemic role in
windowing negotiation is examined in
Chapter~\ref{ch:industry_reconfig}.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:overview_summary}

This chapter has traced the structural evolution of the Tamil film
industry from its silent-era origins through the talkies, the
Dravidian-political phase, the multi-star economy, the 2000s
modernisation, the 2010s capital intensification and the
pre-pandemic baseline of 2019.  It has presented secondary-data
trajectories on theatrical box-office, release volumes, OTT
penetration, and exhibition footprint, and has summarised the COVID
disruption timeline.  Three structural points anchor the chapters
that follow: (i) Tamil cinema entered the pandemic with a strong but
budget-escalated theatrical base; (ii) it was forced into a
digital-acceleration phase that compressed several years of
diffusion into eighteen months; and (iii) the post-2022 equilibrium
is qualitatively different from the 2019 baseline, even where
absolute revenue indicators have recovered or surpassed it.  These
points motivate the empirical analyses presented in
Chapters~\ref{ch:viewership} and~\ref{ch:industry_reconfig}.
